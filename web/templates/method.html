How the model works

A kinematic model, written by hand, checked against closed form solutions wherever one exists. This page states what it represents and, just as importantly, what it does not.

The vehicle

State is the position and heading of the rear axle. That reference point is what makes the equations exact rather than approximate: with no slip, the rear wheel has no lateral velocity, so the velocity vector lies along the heading.

x' = v cos(theta)
y' = v sin(theta)
theta' = (v / L) tan(delta)

Integration is fourth order Runge Kutta at a fixed timestep, with the steering command held constant across each step, which is what a real digital controller does. A test confirms that a constant steering angle traces a circle of radius L divided by tan of that angle, to within a millionth of a metre over two thousand steps.

The steering actuator

The controller emits a commanded angle. The wheels follow it through a first order lag with a slew rate limit, so the steering angle becomes a state of its own.

delta' = clamp((delta_cmd - delta) / tau, -rate, +rate)

Both the time constant and the slew rate are assumptions. No manufacturer publishes them. Because the stability conclusions depend heavily on the time constant, the command line tools sweep it and report the sensitivity rather than quoting a single figure.

Terrain

Side slope adds a lateral drift velocity perpendicular to the heading. The brief for this project describes that drift as proportional to g times sine of the slope angle, which is an acceleration, so the constant of proportionality carries units of seconds and is itself an assumption.

Wheel slip scales forward velocity by one minus the slip fraction, and the yaw equation uses that same reduced velocity, because turning follows actual travel rather than wheel rotation. Slip values by soil surface come from published extension guidance on ballasting.

The implement, and the second measurement

A trailed implement adds hitch angle as a further state, driven by the rolling constraint at its axle.

theta_i' = (1/b) [ v sin(theta - theta_i)
                 + (v_d - a theta') cos(theta - theta_i)
                 - v_d_implement ]

The working edge error is measured against plus or minus half the working width, not against the guidance line. Measuring it against the line would report a perfectly placed wide implement as being half its own width out.

What the results are checked against

Where a closed form exists, the simulation is compared with it rather than with its own earlier output. On a side slope, pure pursuit settles at

e_ss = L_d v_d / sqrt(v_eff^2 + v_d^2)

and the simulation agrees to within about three parts in ten thousand billion. That expression contains no wheelbase term, and the simulation accordingly returns the same offset for every tractor in the catalog.

What this model does not represent

  • Tyre forces, weight transfer and load. The model is kinematic, so it has no notion of grip running out.
  • Articulated steering. Machines that pivot at a frame joint are held in the catalog but refused by the simulator.
  • Implement draft forces and ground contact, beyond a single figure used to check whether a pairing is feasible at all.
  • Headland turns. Every run here is a straight AB line.
